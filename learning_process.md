# Learning Principles & Techniques

## 1. Feynman Technique

**Definition:**  
Make your brain truly understand a topic by explaining it in very simple words, as if you're teaching a little kid.

### Steps
1. Write down the topic name.
2. Explain it out loud using simple language.
3. Find the parts you can't explain clearly.
4. Go back and study those weak parts.
5. Repeat until your explanation is simple and complete.

> **Rule:** If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough.

# Barbara Oakley's Hiker Analogy

Some people learn very fast.

Barbara Oakley compares them to **race car drivers**.

- They reach the finish line quickly.
- But because they're moving so fast, everything becomes a blur.
- They often miss many important details.

Other people learn more slowly.

She compares them to **hikers**.

A hiker takes longer to reach the same destination, but they experience much more along the way.

They can:

- 🌲 Smell the pine air
- 🍃 Touch the leaves
- 🐇 Notice the little rabbit trails

### Lesson

**Slow learners are not worse learners.**

Sometimes they develop a deeper and more complete understanding because they notice details that fast learners miss.

# Focus Mode vs Diffuse Mode

## 1. Focus Mode (Active Thinking)

This is when you're concentrating deeply.

Characteristics:

- Strong concentration
- Logical thinking
- Follows familiar pathways
- Best for careful learning and practice.

Use Focus Mode when:

- Learning new material
- Solving problems
- Reviewing concepts

---

## 2. Diffuse Mode (Relaxed Thinking)

This happens when you're relaxed.

Instead of focusing on one thing, your brain makes broader connections.

Diffuse mode is especially helpful when you're stuck.

While you're relaxing, your brain quietly works on the problem in the background.

### If you're stuck

Don't keep forcing it.

Instead:

- Take a walk
- Rest
- Exercise
- Sleep

Often the answer becomes much clearer.

---

# Deconstruct the Skill

When learning anything new:

## 1. Break the skill into small parts

Don't learn everything at once.

Master each part separately.

---

## 2. Learn enough to self-correct

You don't need to know everything.

You only need enough understanding to notice your own mistakes.

---

## 3. Remove practice barriers

Make practice easy.

- Remove distractions.
- Prepare your workspace.
- Keep everything ready before you begin.

The easier it is to start, the more you'll practice.

---

## 4. Practice for at least 20 hours

The first 20 hours of focused practice usually build basic competence.

Be consistent.

---

# Pomodoro Technique

Study with short focused sessions.

### One Pomodoro

- Focus for **25 minutes**
- Take a **5-minute break**

Repeat several times.

---

# Active Recall

Don't just:

- Read
- Highlight

Instead:

1. Close the book.
2. Look away.
3. Try to remember everything.
4. Explain it from memory.

Your brain becomes stronger when it retrieves information.

---

# Spaced Repetition

Don't study something only once.

Review it multiple times over several days.

Repeated exposure helps move knowledge into long-term memory.

---

# Exercise Helps Learning

Regular physical activity improves:

- Memory
- Focus
- Mood
- Learning ability

---

# Beat Procrastination

When you don't feel like studying:

Just start for **2–5 minutes**.

Usually the uncomfortable feeling disappears once you've begun.

Starting is the hardest part.

---

# Complete Learning Workflow

When learning a new topic:

1. Learn the basics.
2. Break the skill into smaller parts.
3. Use **Focus Mode** to study deeply.
4. If stuck, switch to **Diffuse Mode** by taking a break.
5. Use the **Feynman Technique** to explain the concept simply.
6. Find weak areas and study them again.
7. Use **Active Recall** instead of re-reading.
8. Review using **Spaced Repetition**.
9. Practice consistently for **at least 20 hours**.
10. Use the **Pomodoro Technique** (25 minutes work + 5 minutes break).
11. Exercise regularly to improve learning.
12. If procrastinating, commit to studying for just a few minutes.
13. Remember the **Hiker Analogy**—learning slowly can lead to deeper understanding than rushing.
